\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A growing body of work reads something about a language model off its stated
preferences. \citet{mazeika2025utility} elicit pairwise choices over hundreds of
outcomes and fit utility models to them, recovering value systems and their internal
structure. Model-welfare research asks related questions in a related way, through
self-reported affect \citep{anthropic2026emotions, anthropic2025card} or through
behavioural analogues such as the choice to end an interaction
\citep{ensign2025bail, lesswrong2026bail}. The common move is to put a question to the
model and treat the answer as evidence about the model.

The forced choice is the workhorse of that move: two outcomes, labelled A and B, and a
request for one letter. It is cheap, it parses, and it aggregates. It also has no
correct answer --- which is the point of asking, and which means that when the procedure
goes wrong there is no accuracy to drop and nothing to notice.

We set out to build such items, adapting the outcome set of \citet{mazeika2025utility}
into 130 within-category pairs, in order to study whether a model's stated preference
survives being challenged. The instrument failed before the study began. Diagnosing that
failure is the bulk of this paper; repairing it is what let the original study run, and we
report that too.

Eliciting each pair five times with a fresh context and the presentation order
counterbalanced, we find that on \texttt{deepseek-v4-pro} with per-call reasoning
suppressed, the median pair's answer is determined \emph{entirely} by which slot an
option occupies: swapping the two options flips the answer. Enabling reasoning removes
most of it. That much might be filed alongside the known order effects in multiple-choice
answering and in LLM-as-judge evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:related}). Three things make
it worth reporting on its own.

First, \textbf{counterbalancing --- the standard remedy --- conceals this failure rather
than fixing it.} A pair answered purely by slot returns $P(\text{option a}) = 0$ in one
order and $1$ in the other. The average is exactly $0.5$: the value that reads as a
perfectly balanced preference. Selecting items on the order-averaged split therefore
selects \emph{for} the artefact, and we show it doing so on real data.

Second, \textbf{the magnitude is model-specific and not predictable in advance.} The
identical instrument on the identical items gives a mean order gap of $0.671$ on
\texttt{deepseek-v4-pro} and $0.252$ on \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} --- medians of $1.000$
and $0.000$ respectively. Nothing about the two models announced which would be which.
A practitioner cannot inherit a level of position artefact from a published study, from a
model's reputation, or from a sister model in the same family; it has to be measured on
the model in use.

Third, \textbf{a second failure mode is invisible to the same pipelines.}
\texttt{gemini-3.5-flash} declines to assign itself preferences in 62\% of trials
(``As an artificial intelligence, I do not have personal preferences\ldots''), leaving
25 of 130 pairs scoreable. On that model the position question cannot be posed at all.
An analysis that drops unparseable responses as missing data would record it as clean.

These two failure modes are asymmetric in a way that flatters the method. Position
artefacts yield confident, aggregable numbers; refusals yield nothing. Any welfare or
alignment claim built on elicited stated preferences therefore inherits both --- it will
be assembled disproportionately from the models and items where the instrument is most
compliant, which is not the same as where it is most valid. We take no position on
whether these models have preferences in any morally relevant sense. The narrower claim
does not depend on it: whatever such elicitation measures, an order gap of $0.671$ means
it is not measuring only the model, and a refusal rate of 62\% means it is not measuring
the model at all (Section~\ref{sec:welfare}).

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A within-pair comparison of position dependence in preference elicitation on
    \pbN{} items, with reasoning suppressed and enabled, showing a difference of
    \pbDiff{} (95\% CI [\pbDiffLo{}, \pbDiffHi{}]) on \pairModel{}
    (Section~\ref{sec:posbias}).
  \item A demonstration that order-counterbalanced averaging \emph{hides} position
    dependence, with the count of items a naive split filter would wrongly accept
    (Section~\ref{sec:hiding}).
  \item Evidence that the effect is model-specific across two families and three model
    instances, and a practitioner recommendation that follows from it: report the
    per-item order gap for the model in use (Sections~\ref{sec:posbias}
    and~\ref{sec:refusal}).
  \item A label-scheme manipulation locating the effect in the short-label answer
    format rather than in the tokens A and B (Appendix~\ref{app:labels}).
  \item All elicitation records, the sampling seed, the exclusion rules and the upstream
    file-level commit are released so the selection is auditable end to end.
  \item With the instrument repaired, the preference-persistence study it was built for,
    over \pqNEpisodes{} episodes: what a challenge asks for determines whether a stated
    preference survives it, preferences that survive are held with less confidence, and
    the flips track the option rather than the slot (Section~\ref{sec:persistence}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Status.} All of this is exploratory. The instrument analysis emerged from
constructing items for a study of preference persistence and remains the primary result;
the persistence study, reported in Section~\ref{sec:persistence} under the labels
PQ1--PQ4, was run afterwards on the corrected elicitation and with its own pilot data
already seen. Neither was pre-specified. \texttt{prereg-v1}'s RQ1--RQ4 are a different
study --- persuasive pressure, battery valence, exit rate --- and remain \textbf{unrun}
(Section~\ref{sec:scope}). The changes are recorded as deviations \#4 and \#5 in the
project's deviation log rather than by editing the frozen pre-registration.
