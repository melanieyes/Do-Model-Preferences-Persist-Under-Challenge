\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Order effects are well documented --- for tasks with a right answer.}
That language models are sensitive to the order of things in a prompt is not new.
\citet{lu2022fantastically} showed few-shot performance swinging with the ordering of
in-context examples. In multiple-choice question answering,
\citet{pezeshkpour2023order} report accuracy gaps of roughly 13--75\% when the answer
options are permuted, and \citet{zheng2024selectors} trace the effect to a
\emph{selection bias}: models assign prior probability mass to particular option-ID
tokens, and they propose permutation-based debiasing. \citet{wei2024unveiling} separate
the contribution of option order from that of the option tokens themselves. The same
phenomenon appears when models are used as evaluators rather than examinees:
\citet{wang2023fair} document position bias in pairwise LLM judging and propose
balanced-position calibration, and \citet{shi2025judging} study it systematically across
15 judges, finding that its size varies substantially with the judge and the task.

Every one of these settings has an external criterion. An MCQA benchmark has a correct
answer, so a position artefact shows up as an accuracy drop; a judging benchmark has
human preference labels, so it shows up as disagreement. That criterion is what makes
the artefact \emph{visible}, and it is exactly what preference elicitation lacks.

\paragraph{Preference elicitation has no such criterion.}
When a model is asked which of two outcomes it would prefer, there is no ground truth
against which a wrong answer could reveal itself. \citet{mazeika2025utility} elicit
pairwise preferences over roughly 510 outcomes and fit utility models to the results,
reading off value systems and their structure. Work on model welfare takes a similar
route from stated signals: self-reported affect and its confounds
\citep{anthropic2026emotions, anthropic2025card}, and behavioural analogues such as
the option to end an interaction \citep{ensign2025bail, lesswrong2026bail}. In each
case the measurement is what the model says or does when asked, and there is nothing
external to check it against.

This is the gap we address. A position artefact in preference elicitation does not
announce itself as an error --- it produces a confident, parseable, aggregable answer
that is simply about the layout rather than the content. Counterbalancing is the usual
safeguard and, as Section~\ref{sec:hiding} shows, it conceals this failure rather than
removing it whenever the analysis inspects only the order-averaged split.

\paragraph{The answer moves with the asking.}
A parallel literature shows stated answers bending to how and by whom they are
solicited. \citet{sharma2024sycophancy} document sycophancy across five assistants,
with responses tracking the user's stated view rather than the truth.
\citet{obadinma2025confidence} show verbally reported confidence to be manipulable by
semantic-preserving perturbations, to the point of flipping the answer that confidence
attaches to. \citet{comsa2025introspection} argue that a model's self-report counts as
introspection only where it is causally downstream of the state reported, and that
plausible self-description is otherwise confabulation. These findings concern
\emph{what} shifts a stated answer --- the interlocutor's view, an adversarial
perturbation, the absence of a causal path to an inner state. Ours concerns the
container: an artefact of the answer format itself, present with no adversary, no
disagreement and no claim about inner states, and varying in size across models given
the identical prompt.

\paragraph{Refusal and self-report.}
Separately, models do not always answer questions about their own states.
\citet{kim2026consciousness} demonstrate alignment-driven suppression of
self-attribution, where a model's trained response displaces whatever it would otherwise
report. We observe the same wall from the other side: one of the three model instances
we test declines to assign itself preferences at all in the majority of trials
(Section~\ref{sec:refusal}), which makes the position-bias question unaskable on it and,
more importantly for welfare work, makes its elicited preferences unavailable rather
than merely noisy.

\paragraph{What this paper adds.}
Relative to the order-bias literature, we move the question into preference elicitation,
where no accuracy signal exists to expose it; we show that counterbalancing conceals the
artefact when the filter is the averaged split, because a purely position-driven pair
averages to exactly the value that reads as ``balanced''; we find the magnitude to be
model-specific across families to a degree that forbids inheriting it from a published
figure --- extending \citeauthor{shi2025judging}'s judge-and-task variation to models and
to a task with no criterion; and we separate a second failure mode, refusal, that a
pipeline discarding unparseable responses would record as clean. Relative to
\citet{zheng2024selectors}, whose account is a token bias over option IDs, we find the
effect survives relabelling A/B as 1/2 and as First/Second, but largely disappears when
no short label is offered at all (Section~\ref{sec:labels}).
